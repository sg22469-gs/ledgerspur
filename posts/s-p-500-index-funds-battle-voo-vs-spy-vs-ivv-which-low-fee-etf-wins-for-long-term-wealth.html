The Great S&P 500 ETF Showdown: VOO vs SPY vs IVV

When building long-term wealth, few core holdings match the simplicity and proven performance of the S&P 500 index. Representing 500 of the largest, publicly traded U.S. corporations, this benchmark delivers instant diversification across roughly 80% of the total U.S. stock market capitalization.

However, once you decide to invest in the index, you face an immediate dilemma: Which ticker symbol should you actually buy? The three heavyweights—Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO), SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust (SPY), and iShares Core S&P 500 ETF (IVV)—control trillions of dollars in assets combined. They track the exact same index, yet subtle structural differences, management fees, and share lending mechanisms can alter your net returns over decades.

Whether you are allocating capital inside a tax-advantaged Roth IRA via Robinhood or setting up automated portfolio rebalancing on M1 Finance, choosing the right ETF is critical to keeping fees from silently eroding your retirement nest egg.

In-Depth ETF Comparison: VOO vs. SPY vs. IVV

1. Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO)

Launched in 2010 by Vanguard, VOO quickly became the standard-bearer for low-cost retail index investing. Backed by Vanguard's unique client-owned structure, VOO is designed to keep expenses as low as mathematically possible for standard investors.

VOO operates as an open-end management investment company. Unlike older trust structures, VOO can immediately reinvest cash dividends paid by underlying holdings back into the market, preventing "cash drag." Furthermore, Vanguard engages in conservative securities lending, passing the revenues back into the fund to offset internal operating costs and lower tracking error.

✓ Pros
  • Ultra-low 0.03% expense ratio ($3 per $10,000 invested annually).
  • Open-end fund structure eliminates dividend cash drag.
  • Securities lending revenue passed directly to shareholders.
✗ Cons
  • Lower options market liquidity compared to SPY.
  • Slightly wider bid-ask spreads during off-peak trading hours.

2. iShares Core S&P 500 ETF (IVV)

Managed by BlackRock, IVV is the primary rival to VOO. It directly matches VOO’s 0.03% expense ratio and features an identical open-end fund structure. IVV boasts massive total Assets Under Management (AUM), often trading places with SPY and VOO as one of the largest funds on earth.

Because BlackRock partners closely with zero-commission retail brokerages, IVV is widely available for fractional-share trading down to single-dollar increments on platforms like Webull and Robinhood. For long-term portfolios, IVV and VOO are functionally identical in terms of net performance and dividend yield.

✓ Pros
  • Rock-bottom 0.03% expense ratio matching VOO.
  • Exceptional portfolio tracking precision with low tracking error.
  • Open-end structure permits dividend reinvestment and share lending.
✗ Cons
  • Option liquidity lags behind SPY (though sufficient for retail investors).
  • No inherent structural advantage over VOO for Vanguard account holders.

3. SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust (SPY)

Created in 1993 by State Street Global Advisors, SPY was the very first ETF listed in the United States. It remains the Goliath of Wall Street liquidity. Traded hundreds of millions of times per day, SPY boasts penny-wide bid-ask spreads and the deepest options market in global finance.

However, SPY carries two critical drawbacks for long-term buy-and-hold wealth builders:

  1. Higher Expense Ratio: At 0.09%, SPY costs triple the annual management fees of VOO and IVV.
  2. Legacy UIT Structure: SPY is legally bound as a Unit Investment Trust (UIT). Under UIT rules, SPY cannot reinvest dividends into index stocks as soon as they arrive; it must hold them in non-interest-bearing cash accounts until quarterly payout dates. SPY is also restricted from lending out underlying portfolio shares to earn extra yield for investors.
✓ Pros
  • Unrivaled trading volume and options contract depth.
  • Tightest possible bid-ask spreads for high-frequency trades.
  • The industry benchmark ticker for market professionals.
✗ Cons
  • Expense ratio (0.09%) is 3x higher than VOO and IVV.
  • UIT structure creates dividend cash drag during rising markets.
  • Cannot generate securities-lending revenue for fundholders.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is VOO better than SPY for long-term wealth?

Yes. VOO is superior to SPY for long-term buy-and-hold investors due to its lower expense ratio (0.03% vs 0.09%) and its open-end fund structure. The open-end structure avoids dividend cash drag by immediately reinvesting dividends into index holdings, whereas SPY’s Unit Investment Trust structure holds cash uninvested until quarterly distributions.

Can I hold VOO, IVV, and SPY together in one portfolio?

While you can hold all three, doing so provides zero extra diversification. All three funds hold identical market weightings of the exact same 500 underlying American companies. Holding multiple funds tracking the same index adds unnecessary complexity to your tax accounting and portfolio management. It is best to consolidate into a single low-cost pick like VOO or IVV.

Why does SPY maintain higher trading volume despite higher fees?

SPY was the first S&P 500 ETF introduced to the financial markets, giving it a massive first-mover advantage among institutional hedge funds, market makers, and day traders. Its immense daily option trading liquidity allows institutional investors to execute massive trades without moving the market price, outweighing the minor fee difference for short holding periods.

What is the primary difference between a Unit Investment Trust (UIT) and an Open-End ETF?

A Unit Investment Trust (like SPY) operates under strict legacy SEC rules that mandate fixed portfolio structures. UITs cannot lend underlying shares for revenue, nor can they immediately reinvest cash dividends back into stocks before distribution. An Open-End ETF (like VOO or IVV) enjoys flexible cash management rules, allowing managers to optimize returns via share lending and instant dividend reinvestment.

Final Verdict: Which Low-Fee ETF Wins?

For 95% of retail investors building long-term wealth, VOO (Vanguard S&P 500 ETF) and IVV (iShares Core S&P 500 ETF) tie as the absolute winners. Both funds charge an industry-leading 0.03% expense ratio, utilize modern, tax-efficient ETF structures, and maximize compound growth by eliminating dividend cash drag.

Reserve SPY strictly for short-term swing trading, day trading, or executing high-volume options strategies. For setting up your automated Roth IRA or brokerage portfolio, pair VOO or IVV with low-cost platforms like M1 Finance or Robinhood to let your money compound fully unhindered by excessive management fees.
