Solo 401(k) vs SEP IRA in 2026: Which Retirement Plan Saves Self-Employed High Earners More Money?

For self-employed professionals, freelancers, and small business owners generating six-figure incomes, managing your tax burden is often the single fastest way to increase net wealth. Traditional retirement accounts designed for W-2 employees, such as a standard Traditional or Roth IRA capped at modest limits, quickly run out of room when you are trying to shelter $50,000 or more from high federal and state tax brackets.

Enter the two heavyweights of tax-advantaged self-employed retirement accounts: the Solo 401(k) (also known as an Individual 401(k)) and the Simplified Employee Pension IRA (SEP IRA). Both accounts offer massive potential contribution limits—capping out at $69,000–$70,000+ depending on current IRS inflation adjustments—and both reduce your adjusted gross income (AGI) dollar-for-dollar.

However, the structural mechanics under the hood of these two plans are radically different. Choosing the wrong account can leave tens of thousands of dollars in tax savings on the table each year or saddle you with unnecessary administrative burden. Below, we break down how the math works, compare top custodians like Fidelity, Vanguard, Carry, and E*TRADE, and reveal how to pick the right vehicle for your business structure.

The Core Difference: How Contributions Are Calculated

Understanding which account shelters more money requires looking at your role inside your business. Inside a SEP IRA, you act strictly as the employer. In a Solo 401(k), you act as both the employee and the employer.

1. The SEP IRA Contribution Formula

Because a SEP IRA only accepts employer contributions, your maximum contribution is strictly capped at a percentage of your net self-employment earnings (or W-2 salary if operating as an S-Corporation):

  • Sole Proprietorship / Single-Member LLC (Schedule C): Maximum contribution is approximately 20% of net self-employment income (after subtracting half of your self-employment tax).
  • S-Corporation / C-Corporations: Maximum contribution is 25% of W-2 wages paid to you.

2. The Solo 401(k) Dual-Bucket Advantage

A Solo 401(k) allows you to contribute to two separate buckets up to the overall IRS ceiling:

  • Bucket 1 (Employee Elective Deferral): You can contribute up to 100% of your compensation, up to the annual limit ($23,000–$23,500 base level, plus catch-up contributions if age 50 or older). This is money you can defer right away, even on lower gross earnings.
  • Bucket 2 (Employer Profit Share): On top of your employee deferral, your business can contribute an additional 20% of net self-employment income (or 25% of W-2 salary for S-Corps).

Feature vs Feature: Solo 401(k) vs SEP IRA

Roth Contributions and Tax-Free Growth

Under current rules and broker support, almost all standard Solo 401(k) plans allow for Roth Solo 401(k) employee deferrals. You pay tax on the income today, but all growth and future withdrawals in retirement are 100% tax-free. Furthermore, changes enacted via SECURE 2.0 allow employer matching/profit-sharing contributions to be designated as Roth as well.

While SECURE 2.0 technically legalized Roth SEP IRAs, major major financial institutions have been slow to roll out operational Roth SEP infrastructure. In practice, almost every traditional custodian only supports pre-tax SEP IRA contributions.

Participant Loan Provisions

A major advantage of a Solo 401(k) is the ability to write a loan provision into your plan document. A prototype or custom Solo 401(k) allows you to borrow up to 50% of your account balance (up to a maximum of $50,000) tax-free and penalty-free. You pay the interest back to yourself into your own 401(k) account.

SEP IRAs strictly forbid loans. Taking money out of a SEP IRA before age 59½ triggers income taxes plus a 10% early withdrawal penalty (unless an IRS exception applies).

Administrative Burden & Form 5500-EZ

This is the primary area where the SEP IRA wins on convenience. A SEP IRA requires no annual tax reporting filings to the IRS regardless of how large the balance grows.

A Solo 401(k) requires minimal oversight when starting out, but once total assets across all your Solo 401(k) plans exceed $250,000 at the end of a plan year, you are required by law to file IRS Form 5500-EZ annually. While relatively straightforward, missing this filing carries steep IRS penalties if left unmanaged.

E*TRADE offers one of the most flexible "free" prototype Solo 401(k) options among legacy online brokers. How to Choose: Step-by-Step Insider Strategy

To determine the exact retirement vehicle to open, follow this decision flowchart based on your financial picture:

  1. Do you have or plan to hire non-spouse W-2 employees?
    • Yes: You cannot use a Solo 401(k). You must use a SEP IRA or a traditional small business 401(k). (Note: If you adopt a SEP IRA, you must make equal percentage contributions for all eligible employees).
    • No: Proceed to step 2.
  2. Is your net self-employment income below $300,000?
    • Yes: A Solo 401(k) is mathematically superior. The employee deferral component allows you to shelter tens of thousands of dollars more than a SEP IRA.
    • No: If your income is above $300k, both plans will hit the maximum federal contribution cap ($69,000+). If you prioritize absolute zero administrative paperwork, choose a SEP IRA. If you want Roth tax treatment or loan access, choose a Solo 401(k).
  3. Do you want tax-free growth via Roth contributions?
    • Yes: Choose a Solo 401(k) with a provider that supports Roth deferrals (e.g., Carry or E*TRADE).
  4. Are you past the tax year deadline?
    • Thanks to SECURE Act 1.0/2.0 changes, both Solo 401(k)s and SEP IRAs can be adopted up to your tax return deadline (including tax extensions) for the prior tax year. However, establishing employee deferrals for a Solo 401(k) requires proper paperwork tracking within the tax year.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I have both a Solo 401(k) and a SEP IRA at the same time?

Technically, yes, you can maintain both accounts. However, you cannot double-dip on contribution limits. The overall combined employer limit across both plans is bound by the annual IRS section 415(c) limit ($69,000+ depending on the tax year). Operating both usually adds unnecessary administrative complexity without increasing your total tax-sheltered ceiling.

Which plan saves more money on a $100,000 net self-employment income?

The Solo 401(k) wins by a wide margin. On $100,000 of net Schedule C income, a SEP IRA restricts your contribution to approximately 20% of net earnings (~$18,580). A Solo 401(k) allows you to contribute the full $23,500 employee deferral *plus* the ~$18,580 employer share, yielding a total tax deduction of over $42,000.

What happens to my Solo 401(k) when total assets pass $250,000?

When the total combined balance across all active Solo 401(k) plans under your business reaches $250,000 or more at the end of the tax year, you must file IRS Form 5500-EZ annually. Basic brokerages require you to fill out and file this form manually, whereas modern providers like Carry streamline or automate this reporting requirement.

Can my spouse participate in my Solo 401(k)?

Yes! If your spouse earns income from your business (as a legitimate employee or co-owner), they can participate in the same Solo 401(k) plan. They get their own separate employee deferral limit ($23,500+) plus an employer profit share, effectively allowing a dual-earner self-employed couple to double their household tax shelter to over $138,000 per year.

Final Verdict

For the vast majority of high-earning self-employed professionals without non-spouse employees, the Solo 401(k) is the undisputed winner. It offers higher potential contribution limits at lower income thresholds, provides access to Roth contributions, and offers liquidity through 401(k) participant loans.

The SEP IRA remains a solid, low-friction alternative for business owners earning well over $300,000 who want zero administrative paperwork and do not care about Roth options or plan loans.
