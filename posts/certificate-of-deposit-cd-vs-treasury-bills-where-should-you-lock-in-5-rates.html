Core Differences: Certificates of Deposit vs. Treasury Bills

Before evaluating specific accounts, it is vital to understand the structural differences between these two risk-free asset classes in 2026.

1. Taxation: The State Tax Advantage

Interest earned on bank CDs is fully taxable at the federal, state, and local levels. By contrast, income generated from U.S. Treasury bills is exempt from state and local income taxes, though still subject to federal income tax. If you reside in a state with a high income tax rate—such as California (up to 13.3%), New York (up to 10.9%), or Hawaii—a T-Bill yielding 4.75% can deliver a higher after-tax return than a CD boasting a 5.00% APY.

2. Insurance vs. Sovereign Backing

Bank CDs are insured by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) or National Credit Union Administration (NCUA) up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured institution. Treasury Bills carry no FDIC insurance because they do not need it: they are direct obligations of the U.S. Department of the Treasury, backed by the full faith and credit of the United States government. Risk-wise, both are considered effectively zero-risk assets for individual investors.

3. Early Liquidity Mechanics

If you need your capital before maturity, the mechanisms differ significantly:

  • CD Early Withdrawal: Banks charge a penalty consisting of a specified number of days or months of simple interest (e.g., 90 days of interest on a 12-month CD). This penalty is deducted even if you haven't earned that much interest yet, potentially eating into your principal.
  • T-Bill Secondary Market Sale: T-Bills bought through a brokerage (like Fidelity or Schwab) can be sold at market value on any trading day prior to maturity without paying a bank penalty. However, because bond prices fluctuate inversely with market interest rates, selling early could yield a minor capital gain or loss depending on prevailing market yields.

How to Choose: Insider Tax Math & Strategy Checklist

To maximize your total net return when comparing a CD and a Treasury bill in 2026, follow this step-by-step evaluation process:

Step 1: Calculate Your Tax-Equivalent Yield (TEY)

If you live in a state with income tax, do not compare nominal interest rates directly. Calculate the Tax-Equivalent Yield (TEY) of the Treasury bill to see what CD APY you would need to match it.

Formula:

Equivalent CD APY = T-Bill Yield / (1 - Combined State & Local Tax Rate)

Example: Imagine a 6-month T-Bill yields 4.80%. You live in New York State with a 6.5% marginal state tax rate.
Equivalent CD APY = 0.0480 / (1 - 0.065) = 5.13% APY
In this scenario, a bank CD must offer an APY higher than 5.13% just to break even after taxes with a 4.80% T-Bill.

Step 2: Assess Liquidity Horizons

  • Emergency Cash (0-3 Months): Use a high-yield savings account or a No-Penalty CD from Ally Bank.
  • Near-Term Fixed Outlays (3-12 Months): Purchase 13-week or 26-week T-Bills via Fidelity using the Auto-Roll feature to maximize state tax savings and maintain early-sale flexibility.
  • Long-Term Rate Lock-In (2-5 Years): Bank CDs from institutions like Marcus often offer longer duration options than short-term T-Bills, allowing you to lock in guaranteed returns if federal interest rates decline further.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are Treasury bills safer than bank CDs?

Both options carry virtually zero default risk for average savers. Bank CDs are insured up to $250,000 per depositor per institution by the U.S. government through the FDIC. Treasury bills are backed directly by the full faith and credit of the U.S. government without deposit cap limits. Functionally, both are equally safe, though T-Bills accommodate balances well beyond the $250,000 FDIC cap under a single issuer.

Do I pay state income tax on Treasury bill interest?

No. Interest earned from U.S. Treasury bills, notes, and bonds is strictly exempt from all state and local income taxes. You only pay federal income tax on your earnings. Bank CD interest, conversely, is taxed at federal, state, and local levels.

What happens if I need my money before maturity?

For a standard bank CD, you must pay an early withdrawal penalty set by the bank, which consumes a portion of your earned interest. For a Treasury bill held in a brokerage account (such as Fidelity), you can sell the bill on the secondary market prior to maturity at market value without incurring a bank penalty, though the price received depends on market interest rates at that moment.

Final Verdict: Where Should You Lock In Rates?

Choosing between CDs and Treasury Bills in 2026 comes down to your state tax bracket and operational preferences:

  • Choose Treasury Bills (via Fidelity or TreasuryDirect) if: You live in a moderate-to-high tax state (CA, NY, NJ, MA, OR), want complete state tax immunity, need to invest more than $250,000 safely, or prefer liquidity options via secondary market trading.
  • Choose Bank CDs (via Marcus or Ally) if: You live in a state with zero income tax (FL, TX, WA, NV, TN, WY, AK, SD), want a fixed compound APY with simple online banking access, or want to lock in duration past 12 months without market fluctuation risk.
